Where we started

The mark we replaced.

An italic chrome wordmark with a blue swoosh — the visual vocabulary of the early 2000s. It served its purpose, but it couldn't carry a 2026 foundation across modern channels.

Retired mark · “JCD” wordmark + swoosh · c. 2023

The chrome gradient flattened to gray the moment it hit single-color print, embroidery, or a small screen. There was no square icon for favicons or social avatars, and the polished-corporate styling worked against JCD's honest, craft-first voice. The full diagnostic is below.

The case for a new mark

Six reasons we changed.

The original analysis that justified the redesign — preserved here as the rationale of record.

Observation 01
The style dated us.

Chrome gradient + italic + swoosh-ribbon is the visual vocabulary of 1998–2008 — auto-detailing shops, small-league sports teams, mid-market corporate. It read “established small business,” not a foundation investing in the next generation of tradespeople.

Observation 02
The chrome couldn't survive production.

The silver-to-white gradient that gave the mark its lift disappeared the moment it hit single-color print, embroidery, laser etching, or small-scale screen. The flat trace lost the shine and left a heavy, cramped silhouette.

Observation 03
There was no icon-only version.

Every modern brand needs a reduced mark for favicons, app icons, social avatars, and small signage. The old logo was a wide wordmark-only lockup (1782 × 934) — it simply couldn't compress into a square without becoming illegible.

Observation 04
It didn't signal craft.

JCD funds welders, nurses, carpenters, electricians — people who make things with their hands. The old mark signaled “corporate swoosh,” not “craft.” No visual connection to tools, work, trades, or the people we serve.

Observation 05
It fought the brand voice.

Our tone is editorial-poster × streetwear × Portra documentary — honest, gritty, confident, a little rough around the edges. The chrome-and-swoosh was the opposite: polished, corporate, trying a little too hard.

Observation 06
Equity was low — so the cost was low.

JCD was new: small scholarship count, minimal recognition of the mark, limited print runs. The moment to reset was then, before the old mark lived on thousands of banners, transcripts, and grad photos.

Logo Lab · Round 2

Eight directions we explored.

We ran six directional briefs through two image engines — Nano Banana (Gemini) and OpenAI Image — to pressure-test the field before vectorizing. These are exploration artifacts, not finished logo files.

NB Nano Banana · Gemini OAI OpenAI · gpt-image
Nano Banana01 / 08
Direction 1 · Stencil Monogram
Shop-Wall Stencil

Hand-applied through a real cardboard stencil, safety-orange spray on weathered concrete. Says “we made this, on purpose, with our hands.”

OpenAI Image02 / 08
Direction 2 · Quadrant Seal
Four-Sector Medallion

Four pie slices carry trade, health, safety, and craft icons; a navy medallion locks JCD dead-center. Clean enough for a favicon, rich enough to embroider.

Nano Banana03 / 08
Direction 3 · Tool-Forged Wordmark
Hammer / Clamp / Wrench

J is a claw hammer, C a C-clamp, D an adjustable wrench. Reads as letters first, tools second — playful confidence the stencil didn't have.

OpenAI Image04 / 08
Direction 4 · Industrial Dataplate
Riveted Nameplate

Brushed aluminum, four rivets, stamped serial-number type. Doubles as a “manufacturer's stamp” story — the artifact is the logo.

Nano Banana05 / 08
Direction 5 · Chop-Stamp
Maker's Mark on Leather

Single-ink pressed-leather chop stamp with a circular “EST. 2026” surround. The imperfect pressure is the point — streetwear-adjacent, craft-first.

Nano Banana06 / 08
Direction 6 · Heritage Shield
Chenille Patch on Canvas

Three-panel heraldic shield (mfg / health / trades). Union-worker lineage without feeling costume-y; reads loud from ten feet as a secondary mark.

OpenAI Image07 / 08
Direction 3 · Tool-Forged · On Swag
Screen-Print Tee

The tool-forged wordmark applied as two-color screen print on a cream crewneck — validating the mark at larger scale on fabric.

OpenAI Image08 / 08
Direction 1 · Stencil · On Steel
I-Beam Application

The stencil language on rusted structural steel. It holds on any industrial surface — wall, beam, container — which is why Direction 1 kept its shortlist spot.

The winner wasn't on this wall.

After the round, the team chose a quieter path — a “Tamed Original” that kept the equity of the existing JCD letterforms while fixing the production and small-size problems. That direction was vectorized into the mark you see at the top of this page and across the live brand.
