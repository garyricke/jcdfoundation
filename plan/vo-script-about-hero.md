# About page hero — voiceover script

**Working title:** "A shot with conditions on it"
**Version:** 3 (2026-07-28) — v1 was a historical sketch that pivoted to policy; v2 rebuilt it around the audience; v3 adds the closer that lands on John's vision
**Runs:** **~3:35–3:50** at a documentary pace (518 words). Long for a hero VO — see "If you want it shorter" below for exactly what to cut to reach ~2:45 without losing the spine.
**Where it lives:** click-to-play button in the `about.html` hero, over the silent archival montage
**Audience:** a seventeen-year-old in the Fox Valley who already suspects the trades are a consolation prize

---

## The script

> Somebody has probably told you that the trades are what you do if college doesn't work out.
>
> That's backwards. And the man this foundation is named for would have told you so himself.
>
> John Dunham graduated from West Aurora High School in 1928. Band, track, the drama club. His friends called him Ham.
>
> Then he went to work in his father's factory in Aurora. Running errands. Sweeping the floor.
>
> He was twenty-one when his father made him an offer. John was home from college with a new wife and a four-week-old son, and his father looked at him and said: if you're man enough to have a wife and a four-week-old son, you're man enough to sell on commission.
>
> That's not a soft landing. That's a shot with conditions on it.
>
> He took it.
>
> The company made motorcycle lamps back then. Sidecars. Then car bodies. Then the steel shelving it became known for. He ran it for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on that floor by their first name.
>
> He kept a list he called his rules to live by. Always do the worst job first. Don't look backwards and rehash things. Have confidence, courage, and honesty.
>
> Late in his life he'd visit a classroom, and before he said one word about business he'd walk the room, shake every student's hand, and ask them their name.
>
> And when he put his own money behind young people, he wasn't looking for the highest grades. He was looking for get-up-and-go. Drive. Somebody who'd already proven something to themselves.
>
> When he died, all of it went back to the place that raised him.
>
> This is what it's for.
>
> We fund young people out of twelve Fox Valley high schools, into advanced manufacturing, health sciences, and the building trades.
>
> We won't ask for your grade point average. We won't ask what your parents make. Your parents can be millionaires. They can be on welfare. It isn't our business.
>
> And we don't stop at tuition — because tuition isn't what stops people. What stops people is a car you don't have. Tools you can't buy. Steel-toed boots that cost two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime.
>
> We'll cover that. We'll help you get there.
>
> Here's our end of it. You show up. You show up on time. You show up ready.
>
> And if you try something and find out it isn't for you — that's fine. You found out. That's further than most people ever get.
>
> We're not going to ask you what you've overcome.
>
> We're going to ask what you want to do.
>
> Because that's the question John cared about. He spent the end of his life pushing people to go after the thing they actually wanted — and someone who knew him put it simply: John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life.
>
> That's the part nobody can do for you.
>
> The rest — tuition, tools, boots, the ride there — that part we can do.
>
> So. What do you want to do?

## Clean copy for ElevenLabs

Line breaks are deliberate — they control the pauses.

```
Somebody has probably told you that the trades are what you do if college doesn't work out.

That's backwards. And the man this foundation is named for would have told you so himself.

John Dunham graduated from West Aurora High School in 1928. Band, track, the drama club. His friends called him Ham.

Then he went to work in his father's factory in Aurora. Running errands. Sweeping the floor.

He was twenty-one when his father made him an offer. John was home from college with a new wife and a four-week-old son, and his father looked at him and said: if you're man enough to have a wife and a four-week-old son, you're man enough to sell on commission.

That's not a soft landing. That's a shot with conditions on it.

He took it.

The company made motorcycle lamps back then. Sidecars. Then car bodies. Then the steel shelving it became known for. He ran it for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on that floor by their first name.

He kept a list he called his rules to live by. Always do the worst job first. Don't look backwards and rehash things. Have confidence, courage, and honesty.

Late in his life he'd visit a classroom, and before he said one word about business he'd walk the room, shake every student's hand, and ask them their name.

And when he put his own money behind young people, he wasn't looking for the highest grades. He was looking for get-up-and-go. Drive. Somebody who'd already proven something to themselves.

When he died, all of it went back to the place that raised him.

This is what it's for.

We fund young people out of twelve Fox Valley high schools, into advanced manufacturing, health sciences, and the building trades.

We won't ask for your grade point average. We won't ask what your parents make. Your parents can be millionaires. They can be on welfare. It isn't our business.

And we don't stop at tuition — because tuition isn't what stops people. What stops people is a car you don't have. Tools you can't buy. Steel-toed boots that cost two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime.

We'll cover that. We'll help you get there.

Here's our end of it. You show up. You show up on time. You show up ready.

And if you try something and find out it isn't for you — that's fine. You found out. That's further than most people ever get.

We're not going to ask you what you've overcome.

We're going to ask what you want to do.

Because that's the question John cared about. He spent the end of his life pushing people to go after the thing they actually wanted — and someone who knew him put it simply: John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life.

That's the part nobody can do for you.

The rest — tuition, tools, boots, the ride there — that part we can do.

So. What do you want to do?
```

## What changed from v1, and why

v1 told John's story and then changed the subject to policy. The seam showed. This version makes his life *the argument* instead of the preamble:

- **It opens on the listener, not on 1928.** A seventeen-year-old who's been told the trades are the fallback hears their own experience named in the first sentence. Everything after that is evidence.
- **The spine is a coincidence that isn't one.** John backed students with *"get-up-and-go and drive who'd already proven themselves."* Eighty years later Bob, independently, described the student JCD wants as *"self-motivated, hard worker, has their sights on what they want to do."* Same person, same standard, two lifetimes apart. That's the strongest thing in either source and v1 didn't use it.
- **The commission story does real work now.** A twenty-one-year-old with a newborn being told to sell on commission isn't a charming anecdote — it's the same deal JCD offers. Help with conditions on it. It sets up "here's our end of it" forty seconds before that line arrives.
- **The boots are concrete.** "Two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime" is the single most persuasive thing Bob said all meeting, and v1 didn't have it.
- **It ends on the application question.** The last line is literally what the form asks. The script and the thing it's asking you to do are the same sentence.

## Direction for the read

**The trap is still trailer voice.** Second trap, new to this version: don't get preachy on the second half. The offer section is a list of plain facts, not a rallying cry.

- **Open flat and certain.** "Somebody has probably told you…" is said the way you'd say it to one person across a table. No lift.
- **"He took it."** Full stop, real pause. That's the hinge of the first half.
- **The rules list** — three beats, evenly spaced, no crescendo. They're habits, not commandments.
- **The classroom line is the warmest moment in the script.** Slow down. This is where he stops being a portrait and becomes a person.
- **"This is what it's for."** The turn. Quieter, not louder — the shift is 1928 → now, and it should feel like the narrator leaning in rather than raising their voice.
- **Underplay the negatives.** "It isn't our business" is a fact, not a boast. Flat delivery makes it land.
- **The closer is the hardest part to get right.** "John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life" must not be delivered as a tagline — it's a friend's observation about someone they miss. Say it and then leave it alone.
- **"That's the part nobody can do for you."** Full stop. Longest pause in the piece.
- **The final line is a real question, not a flourish.** "So. What do you want to do?" — the "So" is a separate beat, then genuine curiosity. It should sound like someone waiting for an answer, because that's exactly what the application is.
- **Last lines generally:** slowest in the piece, with full beats between them.
- **Settings:** stability ~50 so it breathes, similarity ~75, style low. High style pushes theatrical, which is exactly wrong.
- **Voice:** male, 40s–60s, warm mid-register. Not a kid narrating an old man's life. Not Bob either — the page speaks in the foundation's voice, not any one person's.

## If you want it shorter

At 518 words this runs close to four minutes, which is a real ask of a seventeen-year-old. I'd rather hand you the cuts than make them for you, because each one costs something specific. **Do not cut the closer** — it's the reason the script exists now. In the order I'd take them:

**Cut 1 — the product list (−28 words, ~12s).** Replace:

> The company made motorcycle lamps back then. Sidecars. Then car bodies. Then the steel shelving it became known for. He ran it for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on that floor by their first name.

with:

> He ran that company for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on the floor by their first name.

*Costs:* the motorcycle-lamps detail, which is charming and tells you the company reinvented itself repeatedly. *Keeps:* everything the audience needs.

**Cut 2 — trim the boots passage (−22 words, ~10s).** Replace:

> And we don't stop at tuition — because tuition isn't what stops people. What stops people is a car you don't have. Tools you can't buy. Steel-toed boots that cost two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime.

with:

> And we don't stop at tuition — because tuition isn't what stops people. Steel-toed boots cost two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime.

*Costs:* the car and tools. *Keeps:* the line that actually lands.

**Cut 3 — drop the drama-club detail (−9 words, ~4s).** "Band, track, the drama club. His friends called him Ham." → "His friends called him Ham."

**Cut 4 — the try-it-and-quit passage (−31 words, ~14s).** Remove the "if you try something and find out it isn't for you" paragraph entirely.

*Costs:* more than the others. This is one of Bob's clearest convictions and it gives permission to a kid who isn't certain. **I'd take this cut last**, and only if you need to be under 2:30.

All four together: **~428 words, about 2:55.**

If you need to go shorter still, the next candidate is the product list in the middle of the offer — *"The rest — tuition, tools, boots, the ride there"* could drop to *"The rest of it — that part we can do."* (−6 words). But it costs the concrete specificity that makes the offer feel real, so I'd exhaust the other four first.

## One thing I left out that you may want in

The documentary records that John's son Jack died in 1979 at forty-eight, and his granddaughter Jean Marie in 1986 **at eighteen**. A speaker says he had "quite a bit of tragedy in his life."

An eighteen-year-old granddaughter, on a page aimed at eighteen-year-olds, next to a rule that reads *don't look backwards and rehash things* — that is extraordinarily powerful material. I didn't use it, for two reasons: it risks reading as emotional leverage on an audience being asked to apply for something, and it involves real grief in a family that hasn't been consulted about this page. **That's Bob's call and possibly the family's, not a writing decision.** If they're comfortable with it, the line more or less writes itself and I'd add it right before "When he died, all of it went back."
