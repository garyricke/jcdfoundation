## The script

> Somebody has probably told you that you must go to a four-year college to have a successful career.
>
> That's backwards. And the man this foundation is named for would have told you so himself.
>
> John Dunham graduated from West Aurora High School in 1928. Band, track, the drama club. His friends called him Ham.
>
> Then he went to work in his father's factory in Aurora. Running errands. Sweeping the floor.
>
> He was twenty-one when his father made him an offer. John was home from college with a new wife and a four-week-old son, and his father looked at him and said: if you're man enough to have a wife and a four-week-old son, you're man enough to sell on commission.
>
> That's not a soft landing. That's a shot with conditions on it.
>
> He took it.
>
> The company made motorcycle lamps back then. Sidecars. Then car bodies. Then the steel shelving it became known for. He ran it for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on that floor by their first name.
>
> He kept a list he called his rules to live by. Always do the worst job first. Don't look backwards and rehash things. Have confidence, courage, and honesty.
>
> Late in his life he'd visit a classroom, and before he said one word about business he'd walk the room, shake every student's hand, and ask them their name.
>
> And when he put his own money behind young people, he wasn't looking for the highest grades. He was looking for get-up-and-go. Drive. Somebody who'd already proven something to themselves.
>
> When he died, all of it went back to the place that raised him.
>
> This is what it's for.
>
> We provide financial assistance for young people graduating from twelve Fox Valley area high schools, pursuing careers in advanced manufacturing, health sciences and the building trades.
>
> We won't ask for your class rank or what you've overcome.
>
> We're going to ask you what you want to do.
>
> Because that's the question John cared about. He spent the end of his life pushing people to go after the thing they actually wanted — and someone who knew him put it simply: John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life.
>
> That's the part nobody can do for you.
>
> So. What do you want to do?

## Clean copy for ElevenLabs

Line breaks are deliberate — they control the pauses.

```
Somebody has probably told you that you must go to a four-year college to have a successful career.

That's backwards. And the man this foundation is named for would have told you so himself.

John Dunham graduated from West Aurora High School in 1928. Band, track, the drama club. His friends called him Ham.

Then he went to work in his father's factory in Aurora. Running errands. Sweeping the floor.

He was twenty-one when his father made him an offer. John was home from college with a new wife and a four-week-old son, and his father looked at him and said: if you're man enough to have a wife and a four-week-old son, you're man enough to sell on commission.

That's not a soft landing. That's a shot with conditions on it.

He took it.

The company made motorcycle lamps back then. Sidecars. Then car bodies. Then the steel shelving it became known for. He ran it for fifty years — and in fifty years, he never stopped knowing every person on that floor by their first name.

He kept a list he called his rules to live by. Always do the worst job first. Don't look backwards and rehash things. Have confidence, courage, and honesty.

Late in his life he'd visit a classroom, and before he said one word about business he'd walk the room, shake every student's hand, and ask them their name.

And when he put his own money behind young people, he wasn't looking for the highest grades. He was looking for get-up-and-go. Drive. Somebody who'd already proven something to themselves.

When he died, all of it went back to the place that raised him.

This is what it's for.

We provide financial assistance for young people graduating from twelve Fox Valley area high schools, pursuing careers in advanced manufacturing, health sciences and the building trades.

We won't ask for your class rank or what you've overcome.

We're going to ask you what you want to do.

Because that's the question John cared about. He spent the end of his life pushing people to go after the thing they actually wanted — and someone who knew him put it simply: John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life.

That's the part nobody can do for you.

So. What do you want to do?
```

## What Bob changed (v3 → v4)

Source: `John edits.docx` — Bob's markup of the John video script, returned 2026-08-26. Six instructions, all applied literally.

**1. New opening line.** The whole first half of the script now argues against a different claim.

| v3 | v4 |
|---|---|
| Somebody has probably told you that **the trades are what you do if college doesn't work out.** | Somebody has probably told you that **you must go to a four-year college to have a successful career.** |

*Effect:* v3 defended the trades against being called a fallback. v4 attacks the four-year default head-on. It's the more common thing a seventeen-year-old has actually been told, and it's a bigger target. "That's backwards" still works verbatim against it.

**2. The eligibility line, rewritten in the foundation's own words.**

| v3 | v4 |
|---|---|
| We **fund** young people **out of** twelve Fox Valley high schools, **into** advanced manufacturing, health sciences, and the building trades. | We **provide financial assistance for** young people **graduating from** twelve Fox Valley **area** high schools, **pursuing careers in** advanced manufacturing, health sciences and the building trades. |

Note "**area** high schools" — this is the safer phrasing, and matches how the list is described elsewhere.

**3. The two "won't ask" lines collapse into one, and "class rank" replaces GPA.**

| v3 | v4 |
|---|---|
| We won't ask for your **grade point average**. We won't ask what your parents make. Your parents can be millionaires. They can be on welfare. It isn't our business. | We won't ask for your **class rank** or what you've overcome. |

**4. Delete everything from the parents'-income line through "We're not going to ask you what you've overcome."** Bob's wording: *"Everything else about your parents' income, what we pay for, all the way to* We're not going to ask you what you've overcome *should be deleted."*

That range removes five paragraphs:

- "Your parents can be millionaires. They can be on welfare. It isn't our business."
- "And we don't stop at tuition… steel-toed boots that cost two hundred dollars on your first morning, before you've earned a dime."
- "We'll cover that. We'll help you get there."
- "Here's our end of it. You show up. You show up on time. You show up ready."
- "And if you try something and find out it isn't for you — that's fine. You found out. That's further than most people ever get."

**5. Delete the closing list.** *"The rest — tuition, tools, boots, the ride there — that part we can do."* The script now goes straight from "That's the part nobody can do for you" to "So. What do you want to do?"

**6. Bob's reason for 4 and 5:** *"What we fund beyond tuition is fluid and may change based on each individual situation. I don't want to set expectations."*

### One thing to confirm before recording

Cut #4 is a range, and two beats inside that range aren't about money:

- **"Here's our end of it. You show up. You show up on time. You show up ready."**
- **"And if you try something and find out it isn't for you — that's fine. You found out."**

Both are Bob's own, near-verbatim from 6/26, and neither sets a funding expectation. They're deleted here because the instruction covers them by position, not because he called them out. Worth one sentence back asking whether he meant them to go — if not, they drop back in after "We won't ask for your class rank or what you've overcome" with no other change.

## Direction for the read

**The trap is trailer voice.** The offer section is now four short lines — plain facts, no rallying cry.

- **Open flat and certain.** "Somebody has probably told you…" is said the way you'd say it to one person across a table. No lift. Don't hit "must" — the sentence is stronger if the narrator sounds like they're just repeating what the kid has heard.
- **"He took it."** Full stop, real pause. That's the hinge of the first half.
- **The rules list** — three beats, evenly spaced, no crescendo. They're habits, not commandments.
- **The classroom line is the warmest moment in the script.** Slow down. This is where he stops being a portrait and becomes a person.
- **"This is what it's for."** The turn. Quieter, not louder — the shift is 1928 → now, and it should feel like the narrator leaning in rather than raising their voice.
- **The three lines after it move fast now.** Eligibility, "we won't ask," "we're going to ask." Don't put weight on the middle one; it's a clearing of the table so the last one lands.
- **The closer is the hardest part to get right.** "John had a wonderful life, because John chose to have a wonderful life" must not be delivered as a tagline — it's a friend's observation about someone they miss. Say it and then leave it alone.
- **"That's the part nobody can do for you."** Full stop. Longest pause in the piece — and now the last thing before the question, so it carries more than it did in v3.
- **The final line is a real question, not a flourish.** "So. What do you want to do?" — the "So" is a separate beat, then genuine curiosity. It should sound like someone waiting for an answer, because that's exactly what the application is.
- **Settings:** stability ~50 so it breathes, similarity ~75, style low. High style pushes theatrical, which is exactly wrong.
- **Voice:** male, 40s–60s, warm mid-register. Not a kid narrating an old man's life. Not Bob either — the page speaks in the foundation's voice, not any one person's.

## Still on the table (Bob's call, not a writing decision)

The documentary records that John's son Jack died in 1979 at forty-eight, and his granddaughter Jean Marie in 1986 **at eighteen**. A speaker says he had "quite a bit of tragedy in his life."

An eighteen-year-old granddaughter, on a page aimed at eighteen-year-olds, next to a rule that reads *don't look backwards and rehash things* — powerful material, still unused. It risks reading as emotional leverage on an audience being asked to apply for something, and it involves real grief in a family that hasn't been consulted. If they're comfortable with it, it goes right before "When he died, all of it went back."
