This is the most useful note I've gotten on this project, and not for the reason you'd think.

You were in the room when most of these questions got answered. You watched the cut and read the page. And you still came away telling me we need to hear the employer on demand — which means the page isn't saying it, whatever is technically on it. If you can't see it, an eighteen-year-old on a phone has no chance.

So I went back through all seven against the page. Here's the honest count.

1 · On the page, but buried

Current demand for the profession

It’s in the tape and it’s on the page — collapsed, three-quarters of the way down, under “How badly do you actually need hygienists?” Dr. Gorenz says there’s a known shortage and that pay has risen because of it. Nobody will ever click it. It should be near the top, in his voice.

2 · On the page, but buried

Characteristics you look for in candidates

Same problem, plus we used the weaker answer. What’s on the page is a hygienist on work ethic. What’s sitting unused in the footage is Dr. Gorenz: “High energy. A drive to want to help people. Patients come first. Interpersonal skills is really big.” That’s the one a kid needs to hear, and it’s from an employer.

3 · Up front, working

Anticipated starting salary

This one’s already front and center — $45/hour, said separately by three of you, plus the benefits package.

4 · On the page, but buried

The role these professionals play in the business

The best two lines of the whole day are collapsed inside an accordion. Dr. Dominguez: “Hygiene is the heart and soul of our practice.” Dr. Gorenz: “Hygiene is the engine that keeps our practice going.” Those belong on the page in large type, not behind a click.

5 · Not on the page

Why the young professionals chose it, and are they happy

Not on the page at all, though it’s on tape. Cassie: “Hygiene was something I stumbled upon, but as soon as I got into it I fell in love with it.” Bethany: “I always wanted to do something in healthcare, but didn’t want the traditional nursing route.” This is the closest thing we have to a kid seeing themselves in the job.

6 · Half up front, half buried

Education — difficulty, cost, and time

Cost and time are up top and clear. Difficulty is buried — and it’s the surprising part: getting in is harder than getting through. One program takes eight students a cohort, another takes 30 out of 100-plus applicants, with two-to-three-year waitlists.

7 · Not asked

Do you think you made the right career choice?

We never asked it. It should have closed every interview.

Two of seven are actually doing their job. Four are real, on the page, and effectively invisible — they're inside a section near the bottom called “Everything we asked,” where nine of the ten questions are collapsed shut. I built that as a reference drawer for you and Jack. I shouldn't have let the best material live in it.

The underlying reason is worth naming, because it answers the bigger question I asked you. The page is built to argue that nobody funds this path — the shortage, the numbers, the missing scholarships. That's a case for the foundation, and it's aimed at an adult. Your seven points are a different page: what the job pays, what it takes, why the people doing it are glad they did. That one's aimed at the kid.

Both are worth having. But if we only get one, I think you're pointing at the right one, and I'd rather rebuild it around your list than keep polishing this version.

Number seven is the only thing we can't fix from what we have. It should have closed every interview, it becomes the last question at the next two shoots, and I'll ask Fairview whether they'll give us a short pickup so this page gets it too.
