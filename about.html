Our Story

He never forgot he started on the shop floor.

John C. Dunham built a manufacturing company from a local Aurora shop into a national supplier — and then gave the whole thing back to the place that raised him. The JCD Foundation is what that looks like pointed at eighteen-year-olds.

That’s the short version. Here’s how it happened. ↓

John C. Dunham · 1920s–2000s
01 · The man

A self-made guy who
treated people well.

Ask the people who knew him and you don't get a resume. You get four things, in this order: he made himself, he outworked everyone, he took care of the people who worked for him, and when he died he gave all of it back.

A self-made entrepreneur. A very hard worker. Treated his employees very well. And as he passed, his entire estate went into helping the community. He gave everything he earned back to the community. Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026. Verbatim except the opening clause — original ran “The John C. Dunham I knew was a self-made entrepreneur.”

That last part is the whole reason this foundation exists. It wasn't a marketing gesture or a tax structure — it was the actual end of the story. A man spent a career building something in the Fox Valley and then handed the proceeds back to the Fox Valley.

He was born in New Jersey in 1910 and his family moved to Aurora when he was three. He went through Aurora grade schools and graduated from West Aurora High School in 1928 — band, track, and the drama club, where the yearbook says his friends called him Ham. It's worth sitting with that for a second: the man this foundation is named for graduated from one of the twelve schools it now funds.

John was introduced to his father's business as a youngster, often running errands and sweeping floors at the factory. Source: Dunham Fund documentary, 03:47 — verbatim narration. See plan/dunham-about-video-transcript.md.

The headline on this page isn't a figure of speech. He started by sweeping the floor of his father's plant, and he stayed close enough to the work that decades later, running the company, he still knew every person in it by name — and they called him John.

02 · The company

Equipto: motorcycle lamps
to steel shelving.

The company began as the Thomas Dunham Company, named for John's father, and it made something you would never guess: motorcycle lamps and sidecars. From there it moved into sports bodies for Ford and Chevrolet, and finally into the product it became known for — heavy-duty steel shelving.

In 1931, John was home from college with a new wife and a four-week-old son when his father made him an offer that reads like a challenge, because it was one: if you are man enough to have a wife and a four-week-old son, you are man enough to sell on commission. He took it. Father and son built the steel shelving line together.

John took the business over after the Second World War and pushed it well beyond the automobile industry it had grown up serving. He was president from 1943 to 1973 and chairman until 1994 — half a century at the head of a Fox Valley manufacturer. Demand eventually outgrew the original plant and moved the company to the old All Steel facility near Highland and Cleveland in Aurora.

He was a good businessman, a good salesman, and a very hard worker. His word was good and his handshake was good, and that's how he preferred to operate. Source: Dunham Fund documentary, ~09:00 — condensed from an unidentified speaker: “Probably the three best things that I remember about John is one, he was a good businessman, two, he was a good salesman and three, he was a very hard worker. He was the kind of person that his word was good and his handshake and that's how we preferred to operate.”

He never stopped being a salesman. He carried the product catalog long after he retired, and he thought selling was everyone's job — the president's most of all. His standing instruction was to under-promise and over-deliver.

03 · Why these three

The three legs of the stool
came from his own life.

People assume the sectors were picked from a labor-market report. They weren't. Advanced manufacturing, health sciences and the building trades are simply the three worlds John C. Dunham actually lived in — two through his company, one through his wife.

LEG 01

Advanced Manufacturing

He ran manufacturing plants. This was the work itself — the floor he started on and the business he took national.

03 · Rules to live by

He wrote down what
he expected. Here it is.

John kept a list he called his rules to live by, and he used it on himself, his family, his employees and his friends. It is not a mission statement and it was never written for a website. It's a list of habits — which is exactly why it still works.

  • 01Good grooming is always important.
  • 02Don't beg, threaten, or assume you're 100% right.
  • 03Always do the worst job first.
  • 04Put your best foot forward.
  • 05Citizenship is very important.
  • 06Avoid suing others if at all possible.
  • 07Loyalty always.
  • 08Use good manners.
  • 09Have confidence, courage, and honesty.
  • 10Don't look backwards and rehash things.

Show up. Do the hard part first. Don't dwell. — that's the whole job.

Read that list next to what JCD asks of the people it funds and there's no daylight between them. We don't ask for a grade point average or a test score. We ask you to show up, show up on time, show up prepared, and do the work of learning. Number three is the one we'd put on the wall: always do the worst job first.

Source: Dunham Fund documentary, 05:57–06:20 — narrated verbatim as John's own list. Transcribed from assets/dunham-about-video.mp4; see plan/dunham-about-video-transcript.md. Worth confirming against a written original (the video implies one exists) before this ships, and worth asking Bob whether the list is already known to the board.

05 · The thing people get wrong

The trades are not
the consolation prize.

Ask what most people get wrong about the trades and the answer isn't about wages or shortages. It's about a sentence people say out loud without ever hearing it.

You can't get a job anywhere else, so you go to the trades. You weren't able to go to college, so you go into the trades. It's like some kids don't go to college, they go into the military — they're looked down upon. No. They're serving their country. They're doing a noble thing. Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026. Verbatim.

What gets assumed

  • The trades are where you land when college doesn't work out.
  • A four-year degree is the real path; everything else is a fallback.
  • Choosing a trade at eighteen closes the door on college forever.
  • If you're any good, someone would have steered you to a university.

What’s actually true

  • It's a noble thing, chosen on purpose — the same way service is.
  • Going to college isn't everything. Plenty of people holding advanced degrees will tell you so themselves.
  • “And they may want to go to college later.” Starting here closes nothing.
  • The kids who are steered are the college-bound ones. That's exactly the gap JCD is built for.

Maybe it’s finally coming around.

Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026 — the MBA line is verbatim (“I'm sitting here with an MBA, big deal. So what?”) but was softened for the public page to keep the voice institutional. Restore it if Bob wants it in.

06 · Who we're looking for

Self-motivated. Hard-working.
Has an idea.

That's the whole profile. Picture the student JCD most wants to reach and you get three qualities — none of them a number.

Self-motivated

Nobody is going to stand behind you. You have to want it before we can help with it.

Hard worker

Shows up. Shows up on time. Shows up prepared. That's the bar every employer named.

Has their sights set

An idea of what you want to do. Not certainty — an idea. That's more than a lot of eighteen-year-olds have, and more than most adults had at that age.

Everybody's going to college. I had no idea what I wanted to do. I had no idea why I was even there. Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026 — verbatim, about his own eighteen-year-old self. Left in first person deliberately: unattributed, it reads as anyone's experience.

And if you don't have an idea yet? Then try something. We're unusually clear on this: starting a nursing program and discovering you hate it is not a failure, it's information. “That's fine. But at least you found out.” Go out to a welding program, decide it isn't for you — “there's nothing wrong with that. But at least you pursued it. Did something. And tried.”

You can't try a four-year degree. That's the point. The paths JCD funds are short enough and cheap enough that exploring one is a reasonable thing to do at eighteen — and checking one off your list is progress, not a wasted year.

07 · What we don't ask

We're an outlier.
On purpose.

Most scholarship applications gate on two things: how well you tested, and how little your family has. JCD asks about neither. Every item below appears on peer applications in this region and appears nowhere on ours.

Grade point average Class rank ACT / SAT score Parents' income What your father does Expected family contribution Your hardship story Citizenship or immigration status Ethnicity
We don't care what your grade point average was. We really don't. You have to have an SAT of 1300, an ACT of 34 — who cares? Some kids just don't do well in that stuff. That's okay. Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026. Verbatim except pronoun — original ran “I don't care what their grade point average was.”

The income question is the sharper break. Peer foundations in the Fox Valley are means-tested — some only consider families near the poverty line. JCD deliberately does not, because that filter misses the actual population: kids in Geneva who want this path and aren't disadvantaged at all, sitting next to kids in Aurora who are.

We don't want to know about your family. Your parents can be millionaires. Don't care. Or they can be on welfare. That's not important. It's about you. Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026. Verbatim, minus a false start.

What we do ask for is one short answer, 300 words or less: what do you want to pursue? Not what you've overcome. Don't tell us about your history of being disadvantaged — just tell us what you want to do. First, because career aspiration is the thing that actually predicts whether this works. Second, because frankly it's none of our business.

Source: Bob Vaughan, June 26 2026 — “Don't tell me about your history of being disadvantaged. No, just, what do you want to do?” and “First of all, it's none of our damn business.” Softened one word for the public page.
